Buying a Used Car NZ: Older Car Checklist So You Don't Get Stitched Up

Cheap older cars can be great value or a money pit. This checklist helps you tell the difference before you pay.

An older car can be a brilliant buy: cheap to insure, simple to fix and fine for getting to work. It can also be a rust bucket with a tidy paint job. If you're buying a used car in NZ, especially an older one, this old car checklist gives you the questions to ask, the checks to do yourself, and the paperwork that protects you, so you know when to walk away.

You don't need to be a mechanic. You need to be methodical, and willing to pay for an independent inspection on anything you're serious about.

First, what's changing with WoFs in 2026

From 1 November 2026, NZTA is changing how often light vehicles need a warrant of fitness (NZTA):

Vehicle age Before From 1 November 2026
Registered before 1 January 2000 (over 26, under 40 years) Every 6 months Annual
14 to 26 years old Annual Annual (no change)
4 to 14 years old Annual Every 2 years (phased in, some from 1 November 2027)

NZTA also says buyers can insist that a light vehicle's WoF is less than one month old at the time of sale. Fewer inspections means less frequent professional eyes on older cars. That makes your own pre-purchase checks more important, not less. WoF inspections will also start checking that no warning lights show for certain driver-assist systems.

What to look for when buying a used car in NZ: red flags

  • The seller rushes you or insists on cash only.
  • No service history, or "my mate did all the work".
  • One panel a different shade, or fresh paint in odd places.
  • A musty, damp smell that comes back after airing.
  • VIN plate damaged, loose or not matching the paperwork.
  • WoF expired, or "it'll pass next week".
  • A price well below similar cars.

One flag isn't always a deal-breaker on a cheap car. Several together usually are.

Rust and flood damage: the NZ-specific problems

Coastal air, gravel roads and wet winters are hard on older cars. Look closely at sills, wheel arches, door bottoms, tailgate edges, jacking points and subframes. Fresh underseal on one side only is worth asking about.

After big storms and floods, damaged cars can reappear "tidied up". Warning signs include silt in seat runners, foggy headlights, water lines inside door cards, corroded plugs under the dash and electrical gremlins. If a flood car is plausible, insist on a paid inspection or walk.

Buying a car privately in NZ vs from a dealer

Private seller Registered motor vehicle trader
Price Often lower Often higher
Consumer Guarantees Act Generally doesn't apply Generally applies
Information Only what you verify Consumer information notice (CIN) on display
If it goes wrong Disputes Tribunal; harder to win Motor Vehicle Disputes Tribunal

Under the Consumer Guarantees Act, a car from a dealer should be of acceptable quality for its age, price and description. A $2,500 high-kilometre runabout isn't held to the same standard as a $15,000 "reliable daily", though.

Check the trader is really a trader

Some people sell cars regularly while advertising as "private". Check the Motor Vehicle Traders Register. Disputes with registered traders can go to the Motor Vehicle Disputes Tribunal.

Buying a used car NZ paperwork: what protects you

  • PPSR check: shows whether a finance company has a security interest in the car.
  • Stolen vehicle check: Consumer Protection recommends checking Police records before buying.
  • Written agreement: price, deposit, date, and faults disclosed in writing.
  • Payment safety: don't send full payment to a stranger's personal account before you've seen the car and its papers.
  • Change of registered person: both buyer and seller must tell NZTA straight away. Do it online at the time of sale (NZTA).

After you buy: the first fortnight

  1. Change the oil and filter if history is thin.
  2. Check coolant, brake fluid and transmission fluid against the handbook.
  3. Book a service and ask about the cambelt if it's due by age or kilometres.
  4. Sort insurance before you drive away.
  5. Keep a folder of the sale agreement, inspection report and receipts.
  6. Pop a small emergency kit in the boot. Our emergency grab bag checklist NZ has the basics.

Frequently asked questions

Does a current WoF mean an old car is in good condition?

No. A warrant of fitness is a minimum safety check on the day. It doesn't assess engine health, gearbox condition, hidden rust under trim, or whether the cambelt is due. Get an independent pre-purchase inspection as well.

Do I have consumer rights if I buy from a private seller?

The Consumer Guarantees Act generally doesn't cover private sales. You may still have options if the seller misrepresented the car, and disputes with private sellers go to the Disputes Tribunal. Buying from a registered motor vehicle trader gives you more protection.

How do I check if money is owed on a car?

Search the Personal Property Securities Register (PPSR). If a finance company has a security interest registered, the car could be repossessed even after you buy it. Consumer Protection also recommends checking the car isn't listed as stolen.

How often will my older car need a WoF?

From 1 November 2026, light vehicles registered before 1 January 2000 (and under 40 years old) move from six-monthly to annual WoFs. Vehicles 14 to 26 years old stay on annual WoFs. Check NZTA for your exact vehicle.

What paperwork do I need when buying a used car in NZ?

Get a written sale agreement with the price and any disclosed faults, check the seller's ID matches the registration details, search the PPSR for money owing, keep the inspection report and get a receipt. Then both buyer and seller must tell NZTA about the change of registered person straight away, which you can do online at the time of sale.
